# FUL.HOUSE — House Inheritance & Continuity

## Product principle

A FUL.HOUSE should outlive a login session, a device and potentially its original owner.

The House is not merely a profile. It is a persistent digital place containing identity, memories, work, relationships, artifacts, economic history and the Butler's knowledge of the House.

This creates a new product concept:

**A digital home can have heirs.**

---

## Core concept

Every House has ownership and continuity rules.

Suggested roles:

- **Owner** — current primary controller of the House.
- **Primary heir** — first intended successor.
- **Secondary heirs** — fallback successors.
- **Trustees** — people authorized to participate in succession verification.
- **Guests / family members** — people with selected access while the owner is active.

The owner should be able to configure succession while alive and change it at any time.

---

## What can be inherited

Inheritance may include some or all of:

- the House itself;
- its public URL and identity;
- rooms;
- furniture and purchased visual assets;
- pets;
- achievements;
- House age and establishment date;
- public portfolio and work history;
- digital products owned by the House;
- VIRT balances if platform rules permit;
- family albums;
- documents explicitly marked inheritable;
- private archives;
- messages prepared for heirs;
- access rules;
- the Butler's House memory;
- provenance and history of important objects;
- owner-created services or storefronts, subject to legal/contractual rules.

Not every connected external account should automatically transfer. LinkedIn, Instagram, Gmail, financial accounts and other third-party identities remain subject to their own ownership and authorization rules.

FUL.HOUSE inherits the **House representation and user-authorized archive**, not ownership of third-party accounts.

---

## The Butler after succession

The Butler remains the Butler of the House.

It should not impersonate the deceased or departed owner unless a future product deliberately and ethically supports a separate memorial-avatar feature.

The preferred behavior is historical continuity:

> This House was created in 2026. This was the owner's first office. These were the projects they chose to preserve. Would you like me to show you the archive?

The Butler knows the story of the House but does not pretend to be the previous owner.

---

## The Archive

Long-lived Houses should develop an Archive.

The Archive stores owner-approved historical material such as:

- yearly timelines;
- major projects;
- work products;
- photos;
- trips;
- milestones;
- important correspondence;
- family stories;
- awards and achievements;
- changes to the House itself;
- selected Butler memories.

The Archive should be navigable spatially rather than feeling like a generic cloud-storage folder.

Potential structure:

- Year rooms;
- family shelves;
- project cabinets;
- sealed boxes;
- memory walls;
- rooms dedicated to specific people.

---

## House Age

Every House should have an immutable creation date.

Example:

**FUL.HOUSE / STAN**

**EST. 2026**

A House that has existed for ten or twenty years should visibly feel different from a new House.

Age can become a status dimension without being purchasable.

Possible age-driven properties:

- architectural patina;
- archive depth;
- historical badges;
- founder-era objects;
- preserved versions of old rooms;
- generational ownership history.

The House should be able to tell its own history.

---

## Inheritance Rooms

Owners may create sealed spaces intended for specific heirs.

Examples:

**For my daughter**

**For my son**

**For my partner**

**For my family**

These rooms can contain:

- letters;
- videos;
- photographs;
- documents;
- private stories;
- project archives;
- instructions;
- digital possessions;
- selected Butler memories;
- family trees;
- unlockable objects.

Possible access conditions:

- specified date;
- heir reaches a specified age;
- verified succession event;
- approval by N-of-M trustees;
- manual transfer while the owner is alive.

The rules must be transparent and auditable.

---

## Living transfer

Inheritance should not require death.

A House may be transferred or shared while the owner is alive.

Examples:

- parent transfers a family House to a child;
- founder transfers a company House to a successor;
- couple creates joint control;
- owner retires and preserves the House as an archive.

This reduces the product's dependence on sensitive end-of-life scenarios and makes continuity useful during ordinary life.

---

## Succession verification

Do not rely on a simplistic "inactive for 90 days = dead" mechanism.

Absence can create dust. It must not transfer property.

Succession should require explicit rules such as:

- owner-triggered transfer;
- recovery/succession key;
- trustee quorum;
- external verified legal process;
- long inactivity plus multiple independent confirmations, only where legally and technically appropriate.

The system should separate:

**House inactivity** from **House succession**.

These are entirely different states.

---

## VIRT and inheritance

If VIRT is a closed-loop platform credit, inheritance can be conceptually simpler than inheritance of transferable crypto assets, but legal and jurisdictional review is still required.

Possible product rule:

VIRT attached to a House may transfer with the House and remain spendable only within FUL.HOUSE.

Inherited VIRT could be used for:

- House maintenance;
- archive storage;
- preservation of premium assets;
- purchases from other residents;
- restoration and expansion;
- family spaces;
- Butler upgrades.

Avoid presenting VIRT as an investment or inheritable financial security.

---

## Pets and continuity

Pets can become emotionally important continuity objects.

A pet may:

- remain with the inherited House;
- remember previous owners through structured House history;
- carry age/status/history;
- interact differently with heirs;
- possess objects or achievements accumulated over time.

A twenty-year-old House with a long-lived digital pet is materially more meaningful than a newly generated profile skin.

---

## Privacy after inheritance

Private information should not become public merely because control changes.

Every archived object needs an access class, for example:

- public;
- family;
- named person;
- heir only;
- trustee only;
- sealed until condition;
- owner-only and non-transferable;
- delete on succession.

The owner should be able to decide that some memories die with the owner.

That is as important as the ability to preserve them.

---

## Data portability

Because inheritance implies long time horizons, FUL.HOUSE should avoid trapping critical family data in proprietary formats.

The owner or heir should be able to export:

- House Manifest;
- archive metadata;
- media references/files where permitted;
- relationships;
- object provenance;
- Butler memory records;
- succession configuration;
- activity history.

Long-term trust requires the ability to leave the platform without losing the archive.

---

## Product narrative

FUL.HOUSE can evolve from:

**a home for your digital life**

into:

**a home that remembers your digital life and can be passed on.**

Potential positioning:

> Built by you. Remembered for you. Passed on by you.

Inheritance is not an MVP requirement, but the underlying data model should support it from the beginning. Retrofitting ownership history, provenance and access inheritance years later would be unnecessarily painful, which is apparently the traditional way software likes to learn about permanence.
